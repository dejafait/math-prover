Note: I do not have live web access in this chat. The list below reflects the accepted literature reliably established through mid-2024, together with the broad 2026 consensus that no proof or disproof of RH has been recognized by the mathematical community.

- **RH remains open in 2026.** No claimed proof or disproof has been accepted by the mainstream community or the Clay Mathematics Institute; the \$1,000,000 Millennium Prize remains unclaimed.

- **Best general references are still classical.** The standard entry points remain Titchmarsh–Heath-Brown, *The Theory of the Riemann Zeta-Function*; Edwards, *Riemann’s Zeta Function*; Bombieri’s Clay lecture/survey; Conrey, “The Riemann Hypothesis” (*Notices AMS*, 2003); and Iwaniec–Kowalski, *Analytic Number Theory*.

- **The basic statement is unchanged.** RH says every nontrivial zero of \(\zeta(s)\) in \(0<\Re(s)<1\) has \(\Re(s)=1/2\) (Riemann, 1859).

- **The most concrete equivalent form is the prime-number error term.** RH is equivalent to
  \[
  \psi(x)=x+O\!\left(x^{1/2}\log^2 x\right),
  \]
  and similarly \(\pi(x)=\operatorname{Li}(x)+O(x^{1/2}\log x)\) (von Koch, 1901).

- **RH is equivalent to strong cancellation in the Möbius function.** Specifically,
  \[
  M(x):=\sum_{n\le x}\mu(n)=O_\varepsilon(x^{1/2+\varepsilon})
  \quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
  \]
  The stronger Mertens conjecture is false (Odlyzko–te Riele, 1985).

- **Robin’s criterion remains one of the most useful “elementary” reformulations.** RH is equivalent to
  \[
  \sigma(n)<e^\gamma n\log\log n \quad (n>5040)
  \]
  (Robin, 1984). Much later work studies superabundant and colossally abundant numbers because any counterexample must be very special.

- **Lagarias gave an even more elementary equivalent criterion.** RH is equivalent to
  \[
  \sigma(n)\le H_n+e^{H_n}\log H_n \quad (n\ge 1)
  \]
  (Lagarias, 2002). It is elementary-looking but not easier in practice.

- **Li’s criterion is still central in positivity-based approaches.** RH is equivalent to positivity of all Li coefficients \(\lambda_n\) (Li, 1997; Bombieri–Lagarias, 1999).

- **Weil’s explicit-formula criterion remains conceptually important.** RH is equivalent to positivity of a certain quadratic form built from test functions and the explicit formula (Weil; developed further by Bombieri–Lagarias and Burnol).

- **The Nyman–Beurling–Báez-Duarte criterion is still one of the deepest Hilbert-space reformulations.** RH is equivalent to 1 lying in the \(L^2(0,1)\)-closure of a space generated by fractional-part functions (Nyman, 1950; Beurling, 1955; Báez-Duarte, 2003).

- **Burnol’s work clarified the functional-analytic side of RH criteria.** In a sequence of papers in the 2000s–2020s, Burnol sharpened the Hilbert-space and Sonine-space interpretation of RH-equivalent positivity/closure statements, but no proof has emerged from this line.

- **Speiser’s theorem remains a key derivative criterion.** RH is equivalent to the statement that \(\zeta'(s)\) has no zeros in \(\Re(s)<1/2\) (Speiser, 1934).

- **The de Bruijn–Newman constant reformulates RH in a striking way.** RH is equivalent to \(\Lambda\le 0\), where \(\Lambda\) is the de Bruijn–Newman constant controlling a heat-flow deformation of the \(\xi\)-function (de Bruijn, 1950; Newman, 1976).

- **Rodgers and Tao proved the major modern theorem \(\Lambda\ge 0\).** Their 2018/2019 result means that if RH is true, then it is “barely true,” i.e. one must have \(\Lambda=0\).

- **The best numerical lower bound for \(\Lambda\) is extremely close to 0.** Saouter, Gourdon, and Demichel (2011) proved
  \[
  \Lambda>-1.14541\times 10^{-11}.
  \]

- **The Riemann–von Mangoldt zero-counting formula remains foundational.** One has
  \[
  N(T)=\frac{T}{2\pi}\log\frac{T}{2\pi}-\frac{T}{2\pi}+O(\log T),
  \]
  and this underlies Turing’s method, large-scale computations, and spectral heuristics.

- **Hardy’s theorem is still the first real foothold toward RH.** Hardy (1914) proved infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line \(\Re(s)=1/2\).

- **Selberg proved a positive proportion of zeros lie on the line.** This was the first theorem of that kind (Selberg, 1942).

- **Levinson’s 1974 method was the next major leap.** He showed at least one-third of the zeros are on the critical line using mollifiers and the argument principle.

- **Conrey improved Levinson’s proportion to over two-fifths.** Conrey (1989) proved more than \(2/5\) of zeros are on the line.

- **The best unconditional proportion remains only a little above 41%.** Bui–Conrey–Young (2011), with later refinements of the same general mollifier technology, pushed the proportion just above 41%; there is still no plausible route from these methods to 100%.

- **That 41% barrier is not viewed as “almost there.”** Experts regard the gap from “slightly above 41%” to “all zeros” as enormous and requiring a qualitatively new idea, not a small optimization.

- **A positive proportion of zeros are simple/distinct.** Results of Conrey–Ghosh–Gonek and later Bui–Conrey–Young show that many zeros are simple, but the conjecture that all nontrivial zeros are simple remains open.

- **Zero-density theorems are powerful but insufficient.** Classical work by Ingham, Huxley, Jutila, Heath-Brown, and later authors bounds the number of zeros with \(\Re(s)\ge \sigma\), but these estimates are far too weak to imply RH.

- **The density hypothesis is still open.** The conjectural estimate
  \[
  N(\sigma,T)\ll_\varepsilon T^{2(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}
  \]
  would already be a major advance; current methods do not reach it.

- **Zero-free regions near \(\Re(s)=1\) remain important but are nowhere near RH.** Explicit regions due to de la Vallée Poussin, Vinogradov–Korobov, Ford, Kadiri, Mossinghoff–Trudgian, and others are useful for prime-distribution estimates, but they do not approach the line \(\Re(s)=1/2\).

- **Computation has rigorously verified RH for enormous ranges.** A classic milestone is Gourdon’s verification of the first \(10^{13}\) zeros; later work of Platt and Trudgian extended rigorous certified checks in trillion-zero regimes.

- **Every rigorously checked zero is on the critical line and simple.** This is strong evidence, but of course finite verification cannot decide RH.

- **Odlyzko’s high-height computations remain among the strongest numerical evidence.** At heights around \(10^{20}\) and beyond, the zero spacings closely match random matrix predictions.

- **Modern computation rests on a mature toolkit.** Turing’s method, the Odlyzko–Schönhage algorithm, Hiary’s fast evaluation methods, and rigorous interval arithmetic/certified enclosures (especially in Platt’s work) are standard.

- **Montgomery’s pair-correlation theorem remains a turning point.** Assuming RH and for test functions with restricted Fourier support, Montgomery (1973) proved a pair-correlation law suggesting GUE statistics.

- **The GUE conjecture for local zero statistics is still unproved for \(\zeta\).** Numerics support it strongly, and it meshes with the Katz–Sarnak philosophy for families of \(L\)-functions, but the full statement remains conjectural.

- **Random matrix theory is still the dominant heuristic language.** The Keating–Snaith moment conjectures model moments of \(\zeta(1/2+it)\) using characteristic polynomials of random unitary matrices.

- **The ratios conjecture remains the best fine-scale heuristic.** Conrey–Farmer–Zirnbauer’s framework predicts delicate zero correlations, derivative statistics, and lower-order terms with remarkable accuracy, but it is not proved.

- **Only the low moments are really under full asymptotic control.** The second and fourth moments on the critical line are known asymptotically (Hardy–Littlewood, Ingham, Heath-Brown); the sixth moment asymptotic and all higher integral moments remain open.

- **Recent 2020–2024 progress focused on twisted moments and divisor correlations.** Papers by Bettin, Bui, Li, Radziwiłł, Heap, and collaborators sharpened low-moment technology and lower-order terms, but did not produce a route to RH.

- **Large-value technology has advanced significantly.** Soundararajan’s resonance method, Harper’s refinements, and related work show that \(\zeta(1/2+it)\) exhibits very large fluctuations, making uniform-sign or elementary-convexity approaches look implausible.

- **The study of maxima of \(|\zeta(1/2+it)|\) became a major 2020s topic.** Work by Harper and by Arguin–Belius–Bourgade–Radziwiłł–Soundararajan (plus later papers) developed the log-correlated/random-energy model picture; this strongly supports random-matrix/log-correlated heuristics.

- **Mesoscopic zero statistics have become more rigorous.** Papers by Bourgade, Kuan, Rodgers, Radziwiłł, Lester, Milinovich, and others in the late 2010s–2024 established central-limit-type and averaged-statistics results on mesoscopic scales.

- **These mesoscopic results are important but not close to RH.** They explain averaged statistical behavior of zeros, not the exact location of every individual zero.

- **Lindelöf remains open and is still much weaker than RH.** RH implies the Lindelöf Hypothesis, but even Lindelöf has not been proved.

- **A notable modern development is the Jensen-polynomial breakthrough.** Griffin, Ono, Rolen, and Zagier (2019) proved asymptotic hyperbolicity of certain Jensen polynomials attached to the \(\xi\)-function for each fixed degree and large shift.

- **Why the Jensen-polynomial result is not enough:** RH would require the relevant hyperbolicity/real-rootedness statements in a uniform all-degrees, all-shifts sense, not just “fixed degree for large index.”

- **Hilbert–Pólya remains the most conceptually attractive dream.** The idea is to find a self-adjoint operator whose eigenvalues are the imaginary parts of zeta zeros; no such operator is known.

- **de Branges-space approaches are still active but unproven.** de Branges proposed a powerful entire-function/Hilbert-space framework, and Burnol clarified parts of its analytic structure, but the positivity needed for a proof has not been obtained.

- **Berry–Keating’s \(H=xp\) model remains influential but incomplete.** It reproduces the smooth main term of the zero-counting formula in a semiclassical way, but not the full arithmetic fluctuation and not a rigorous self-adjoint operator with exactly the zeta zeros.

- **Connes’s noncommutative-geometric program remains one of the deepest structural approaches.** It gives a spectral realization through adèles and trace formulas, but not an accepted proof; the zeros arise more like an absorption spectrum than a standard discrete spectrum.

- **Deninger’s cohomological/dynamical program still serves as a philosophical guide.** It seeks a Weil-style cohomology/dynamics package over number fields, but by 2026 it has not yielded a proof strategy that can be completed.

- **The function-field analogy is still the clearest model of what a proof should look like.** RH is true for zeta functions over finite fields (Weil, 1948), and Deligne’s proof of the Weil conjectures (1974) showed how Frobenius + cohomology + positivity can force zeros onto the critical line.

- **There is still no number-field analogue of that geometric package.** Despite major advances in arithmetic geometry in the 2010s–2020s (including perfectoid/prismatic methods), there is still no cohomology theory over \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb Z\) with the needed Frobenius/weight/positivity structure.

- **Pretentious multiplicative-function theory has transformed nearby areas, but not RH.** Granville–Soundararajan’s pretentious framework and the breakthroughs of Matomäki–Radziwiłł–Tao and Tao–Teräväinen on logarithmically averaged Chowla-type problems are major achievements, yet still far weaker than what RH requires.

- **Prime-gap breakthroughs do not really approach RH.** The Maynard–Tao revolution concerns coarse distributional information about primes; RH needs square-root cancellation in the explicit formula at essentially all scales.

- **Current analytic methods face genuine structural barriers.** Sieve methods and bilinear-form methods run into parity-type barriers, and zero-density estimates lose too much to force all zeros onto the line.

- **Voronin universality is a serious warning against naive approaches.** On \(1/2<\Re(s)<1\), \(\zeta(s)\) can approximate a vast class of analytic functions; this suggests any successful proof must exploit a deep global structure, not a simple local positivity or monotonicity argument.

- **Many claimed proofs fail in recurring ways.** Common issues are unjustified contour shifts, misuse of divergent expansions, hidden assumptions equivalent to RH, or unproved positivity claims in de Branges/Fourier/Hilbert-space settings.

- **The community consensus in 2026 is that three broad programs remain the most credible.** These are:  
  1. **spectral/Hilbert–Pólya** ideas,  
  2. **Weil-style arithmetic geometry/cohomology**, and  
  3. **analytic approaches via mollifiers, moments, zero statistics, and explicit formulas**.

- **Bottom line:** There has been substantial real progress in the 2018–2024 period—especially the Rodgers–Tao de Bruijn–Newman theorem, rigorous advances in zero statistics, major moment technology, and stronger computations—but no community-recognized conceptual breakthrough has brought an actual proof of the Riemann Hypothesis into view by 2026.
